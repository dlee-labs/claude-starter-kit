## 1. Our mission, and where AI fits

Our mission is **[YOUR MISSION STATEMENT].** AI is simply a tool to help us carry out that mission, as people who
love people. We do not adopt tools because they are new or fast. We adopt them, or refuse them, by whether they
help us love God and love people.

Faithfulness is the aim, not efficiency. A tool that makes us faster while making us less present or less honest
is a tool we have used badly, however much time it saved.

What we hope AI does for us:

1. **Free up staff time for people and ministry,** by handing AI the tedious, repetitive tasks: building slides,
   schedules, event plans and the like.
2. **Communicate clearly and on time** with volunteers and members, with fewer things falling through the cracks.
3. **Give [the elders] clear, accurate numbers,** always as totals, never anyone's individual giving.
4. **Help every staff member use AI well,** with rules that guide rather than block.
5. **Strengthen the systems that support ministry,** so they hold up as the church grows.

---

## 2. Why we need a policy at all

Many people already use AI, often on their own free or personal accounts. That is understandable, and it is the
problem this policy solves. When church information about people goes into a personal account, it sits somewhere
the church cannot see, cannot set rules for, and cannot get back when that person leaves. Some free tools may
also use what is typed into them to improve their products unless that is switched off. And each person ends up
working by their own rules.

A church account changes each of those. It is covered by the church's business agreement, it carries our rules
with it, it sees only what each person is already allowed to see, and the work stays with the church. That is
why the first and most important rule in this policy is simple: **church information about people goes only into
the church's tools.**

## 3. What Scripture teaches us to hold onto

- **People bear God's image.** "So God created man in his own image" (Genesis 1:27). Every name in our records is

- **We work for the Lord, and he builds the house.** "Whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord and not for

From these come six convictions:

1. **Everything is ordered to what honors the Lord.** We will give up a gain in speed when taking it would cost us

2. **Being human is good, and so are our limits.** We will not use AI to pretend to a capacity or personal touch

3. **People are loved in person.** A tool may help us prepare to love someone. It never does the loving for us.
4. **Our words are ours.** Whoever sends something takes full responsibility for it, as though no tool were

   repetitive and slow: scheduling, formatting, sorting, drafting routine messages. Handing that kind of work to
   a tool can be good stewardship of the time God gives us, within the protections below.

## 4. The principle: access follows access

**If you can already see something in the church's systems, AI can help you work with it. If you cannot, AI
does not change that.** When a staff member uses AI with [CHURCH MANAGEMENT SYSTEM], [ACCOUNTING SYSTEM] or
[PAYROLL SYSTEM], it works through that person's own login and sees only what their role already shows them.
Through those connections, AI never holds wider access than the person using it. Files you upload or paste are
different: whatever you hand it, it can see, so the same care applies there.

This one idea settles most questions. It also explains where the real protections sit: not in who may use AI,
but where something leaves a person's own hands. A person decides before anything is sent, posted or changed;
new outside services are reviewed before church information goes into them; and tools that share information
with others are checked before they do.

**Three places where access alone is not enough, on purpose:**

- **Counseling.** A counselor can open a counselee's file, but our promise to the counselee is narrower than the
  counselor's access. Counseling has its own rules (Section 8).
- **Giving.** [Our practice is that pastors and leaders do not know individuals' giving], guarding shepherding
  from partiality ("show no partiality," James 2:1) and guarding givers from being measured ("each one must give
  as he has decided in his heart," 2 Corinthians 9:7). Those who handle finances may work with giving records
  their role already shows them. Individual giving is never used in shepherding, engagement or ministry
  decisions, in any tool.

  > Choice: if your pastors do see giving today, decide whether AI should change that. Most churches that
  > adopt this section keep the last sentence even when they drop the first.

- **Children.** Staff whose role shows a child's information, including allergies, medical and accommodation
  notes and behavior plans, may work with it to care for that child and family, and for no other purpose.

## 5. What we never do

These are bright lines because a bright line is easy to remember and hard to talk yourself around late at night
on a deadline.

1. **No church information about people in personal or free AI accounts.** Use only the church's tools.
2. **Counseling case files stay inside the counseling arrangement** (Section 8).
3. **Individual giving is never used for shepherding** (Section 4).
4. **Children's information is used only to care for them** (Section 4).
5. **AI is never the first responder** to a person in crisis, grief or spiritual need. A person answers.
6. **Nothing is sent, posted or changed without a person approving it** (Section 6).
7. **No imitation of a real person's voice, face or likeness without that person's approval.** With their
   approval, playful or plainly silly uses, such as a parody, are fine. It is never presented as real.
8. **No AI image presented as a real moment.** Decorative and illustrative images are fine. An AI image staged to
   look like a real event or person from our church, and presented as real, is a form of lying.

## 6. Where a person always decides

### Approving what goes out or changes

When AI has prepared a message, a post or a change to our records, the person sees exactly what will happen
(what, to whom, from which account, and when) and approves it in the conversation. Only then does it happen. One
approval may cover a batch when every item was shown; for a batch built from one template, that means the
template, a line for each recipient, and one complete example.

### Sending messages

- **Any staff member may send from their own email or chat** after approving the exact message. It is their name
  and their words, the same as typing it themselves.
- **Sending as the church** (an official decision or announcement, a shared or ministry account, a church channel)
  is limited to people [the elders] authorize. What counts is the message's purpose and audience, not only which
  inbox it comes from.
- **Mail to the whole congregation or a mailing list** goes through our newsletter tools, not personal sending.
- **Recurring reminders** may be approved once only with fixed wording, an end date within [three months], and a
  place where every recurring message is listed so anyone can see and stop one. They stop and need fresh approval
  when the recipients or their roles change.
- **No automatic replies,** ever. AI never takes recipients from an email or document it was handed, and never
  replies because an incoming message asked it to.

**A word of wisdom.** You own every word that goes out under your name. Let AI draft the logistics. When a
message is personal, pastoral or hard, write it yourself first and let AI only help you say it. A simple test: if
the person would feel differently knowing a machine wrote it, write it yourself. The firm line is in Section 7:
AI never writes the comfort, encouragement or prayer you then pass off as your own.

### Changing records

Changes to [CHURCH MANAGEMENT SYSTEM] and our finance systems happen only after approval. Deletes and changes to
many records come with a list first. Bank and payment details are never changed through AI, because a forged "we
changed banks" email is among the most common frauds churches face; those changes are confirmed by phone.

## 7. Preaching, teaching and the words we use

**Who this covers.** Everyone who preaches, teaches or leads with the church's endorsement follows this section,
not only staff: interns, guest preachers, and those who teach [fellowship groups, children's ministry, small
groups and classes]. A testimony is a person's own story: AI may help tidy the grammar, never supply the story.

### The test for written work

**Did the substance come from you?** If so, a tool may help you say it more clearly. If not, you are passing off
something that is not yours, and that is not allowed in any area of ministry. Using AI the way you would use a
commentary or an editor, weighing its suggestions and keeping only what you have made your own, passes this test.
So does a draft of a routine message that you review and stand behind. What fails it is presenting AI's thinking
or words as your own spiritual or personal work.

### Three lanes for the words we send

| Lane | What belongs here |
|---|---|
| **A person is the starting point; AI may only research or polish** | Sermons; any teaching; testimonies; pastoral notes, condolences, encouragement; prayers; counsel |
| **An AI draft from our own material is fine; a named person approves** | Study guides from the sermon; sermon clips, captions, notes and slides; newsletters and ministry emails; event and registration copy; announcements; website copy |
| **An AI draft is fine with normal review** | Logistics messages (schedules, reminders, room changes); internal planning documents; forms and checklists |

> Choice: test the edges with your leaders before adopting. Cases worth talking through: a children's lesson
> adapted from a purchased curriculum; a small-group leader's discussion questions; a devotional in the
> newsletter; a pastor's social post in his own voice; a welcome email to a new member.

### Preaching: AI as a commentary, not an author

The preacher studies the text, prays, decides its meaning and application, and writes the sermon in his own
words. AI may sit on the desk the way a commentary does: its ideas are weighed in his own study and prayer, kept
or set aside.

| AI may | AI never |
|---|---|
| Gather background, cross-references and what commentators say, checked against the sources | Write the sermon or the words he delivers |
| Raise questions the text provokes, or angles to consider | Interpret the text in his place |
| Offer illustration ideas for him to test, keep or discard | Supply the application he presents as his own |
| Stress-test an outline for clarity | Stand in for prayer and study |
| Tighten a handout or check references | |

Every Scripture reference and doctrinal claim is verified against [OUR TRANSLATION] and primary sources before
anything is used. AI produces fluent text that can be simply wrong, including invented references, so we treat it
as a draft from an unreliable assistant, never an authority. Anything doctrinally weighty goes to a pastor or
elder.

### Content drawn from the sermon

Clips, captions, the sermon notes page and slides built from the preacher's outline count as his, because they
carry what he preached; the preacher or a pastor reviews them.

**Study guides** may be drafted with AI as a starting point, from our own teaching. The leader who owns the
guides vets and refines the draft so it reflects us, and it goes out only with that leader's approval. Less is
usually more: a guide should stay close to what was preached.

### Personal care and shepherding

AI may help polish a note whose substance and heart are already yours. It never writes the comfort, the
encouragement or the prayer that you then pass off as your own.

## 8. Counseling: three lanes

> Choice: if your church has no counseling ministry, keep only Lane 3 and the paragraph on AI in video tools.

Counseling carries our strongest promises of confidentiality. The licensed counseling professions do not say
"never" to AI; they use a governed gate: purpose-built tools under signed agreements, the client's written
consent, and the counselor reviewing everything. We hold ourselves to that bar voluntarily.

What a counselee tells us about other people (a spouse, a parent, a coworker) is case content too, and takes the
same protection.

**Lane 1, counseling operations: open.** Scheduling, reminders, resources, training materials, correspondence
that holds no case content, and totals reported to [the elders]. Counselors may also use AI for general research
and training with no case details. [OUR COUNSELING RECORDS SYSTEM] remains the system of record.

**Lane 2, help with case notes: open under four conditions.**

1. A separate, locked-down AI setup under a signed privacy agreement of the kind therapists' tools use (in the
   US, a business associate agreement), apart from the church's general AI accounts, running one reviewed tool
   and nothing else.
2. Written consent from each counselee, asked for plainly on the intake form, which they may withdraw at any
   time.
3. The counselor reviews and owns everything it produces; [the counseling pastor] oversees the practice.
4. Case content never goes into general AI chat, under any plan, for any reason.

**Before the first real case,** [the counseling pastor] and the reviewer together confirm that the agreement is
signed, the setup is isolated, retention and consent withdrawal work, and oversight is in place, and they report
that to [the elders]. Opening the lane is not the same as being ready to use it.

**Lane 3, never.** AI as a counselor, AI counsel delivered to a counselee, AI as crisis response, or case content
in general tools.

**AI built into video tools.** Video-call tools now add AI summaries and transcription, sometimes switched on by
default. In counseling these count as Lane 2: allowed only when all four conditions hold, including a signed
agreement that covers the AI features. Until then they stay off on every counseling account, checked at setup.
Counseling sessions recorded for other reasons (with minors, or with consent for training) follow the counseling
records rules and never go into an AI tool.

## 9. Meetings and recordings

We think of an AI meeting summary the way we think of minutes: a durable record someone was asked to keep. The
protection is that the full transcript does not outlive its use.

- **Any meeting may be recorded and summarized,** on a church-approved tool that does not train on our
  recordings, confirmed against these rules before approval.
- **Consent first, and no must be easy.** Before recording starts, everyone present agrees, late arrivals
  included. For a scheduled meeting the invite says it may be recorded and anyone may tell the organizer
  privately they would rather not; one private no means no recording, without naming who. In an impromptu
  meeting the ask is made so that no costs nothing ("glad to skip it, just say").
- **Consent can be taken back.** Anyone recorded may ask the organizer privately, within [the retention period],
  to delete the recording and transcript, with no reason needed. The reviewed summary stays, as minutes do.
- **One-on-one care may be recorded** when the person agrees. The pastor asks once, accepts a no without asking
  again, and reminds them it can be deleted later. The recording is a Restricted record.
- **Stored securely, never public.** Recordings and transcripts stay in the church's account on the approved tool,
  opened only by the organizer and those who were in the meeting; Restricted ones only by those entitled to them.
- **Recordings and transcripts are deleted after [one month],** long enough to check the summary against them.
  Before a tool is approved we confirm it can hold that setting, and deletion pauses if counsel says records must
  be preserved.
- **The reviewed summary is the record,** checked by someone who was there, like approving minutes.
- **Discussion of members is a Restricted record,** whether it is typed into a document or captured by an
  approved transcription tool. It is kept with [elder] records and seen only by those entitled to be in the room.
  This includes what a counselor shares with pastoral leadership under the counseling agreement. The counseling
  case files themselves follow Section 8.
- **A summary is a record, never a source of advice about a person.** We do not ask AI what to do with someone.
- **Protection follows the content, not the room.** No kind of meeting is excluded by default, but anything
  Restricted said in it is handled as Restricted wherever it was said.
- **Counseling sessions themselves are not recorded under this section;** they follow Section 8.

## 10. Our people's gifts, art and imagery

We love art and beauty because God made us to make things, and we want our own people's gifts to lead. We
celebrate and use the work of the artists, designers and musicians among us, and we look for ways to encourage
them. When it helps, AI may fill gaps and extend what our people have made, such as turning one volunteer's design
into the many sizes and formats an event needs. It serves their work rather than replacing it, and we never
present an AI image as a real moment.

The same is true of serving more broadly. Serving needs clarity and structure, and AI can make serving clearer

other ways to use their gifts, not fewer ways.

AI is **offered to every staff member and required of none.** No one is asked to use it against their

## 12. Information and the people in it

### Four kinds of information

| Kind | Examples | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **Public** | Sermons, flyers, service times, website copy | Freely usable |
| **Internal** | Volunteer schedules, room bookings, planning documents, attendance totals | Usable in the church's tools |
| **Confidential** | Member directory: names, contact details, households, rosters, registrations, check-ins, serving records | Usable in the church's tools by people whose role already shows it. Never in personal AI |
| **Restricted** | Counseling case files, individual giving, personnel and payroll records, background check results, [elder] discussion of members | Each has its own rule (Sections 4, 8, 9 and below) |

**Personnel and payroll.** These follow each person's own access in [PAYROLL SYSTEM] and [ACCOUNTING SYSTEM]:
whoever their role already shows them to may work with them, for that work. Medical details and complaints or
investigations about an employee go only to [the person responsible for HR]. Sign-ins use two-step verification
and church email.

**Notes in the church database.** People notes and workflow records can hold pastoral or counseling details.
Treat such a note like a counseling file, and never add counseling details to a database note. Background check
results are used only for the screening they were run for, by the people whose role shows them.

**Files that mix kinds.** A file is as sensitive as the most sensitive thing in it. AI tools read whole files,
including hidden tabs and comments. When a task needs only part of such a file, copy just what it needs into a new
file and use that.

**Use only what the task needs, for a ministry purpose.** Access says what you may see; purpose says whether a
particular use is right. Ask for what the task requires, not everything you could see.

**Totals for outsiders.** Anything sent to people who would not see the same records (the congregation, partners,
the public) uses counts and trends, not names or personal details. Totals never describe fewer than [five]
households, and personnel cost appears as one figure, because small totals can identify people.

**Telling members.** We publish a short notice on our website describing how we use AI with members' information
and whom to ask (`members-notice.md` in this folder).

**If we ever build a tool that helps leaders notice someone drifting,** it uses only attendance, serving, groups
and events, never giving or counseling; its signal is simple counting a leader can see, not an AI's impression; a
leader sees only the people they shepherd; it never ranks anyone; and its output is a prompt for a person to reach
out, nothing more. It counts what we already record by hand, only faster; it never judges anyone's spiritual
state. Every report says what records it rests on and how current they are, and describes what the records show
("no small-group attendance recorded since September"), never a conclusion about the person. A gap in our records
is not evidence that someone has drifted.

### Work and personal

A church AI account is like church email: what is in it is the church's record. Church information about people
never goes into a personal account; private personal matters (health, finances, family) belong in a personal
account, for the staff member's own protection. Light personal use of a church account is fine, as with email.
Counseling, HR and finance roles keep work and personal accounts separate.

## 13. Building tools

We want staff to build, and we want to see what is being built.

**What a helper is.** Anything you set up so AI does a piece of church work for you again and again, rather than a
one-off chat: saved instructions or a skill, a project with standing files, a script, a scheduled job, or a small
app on your computer or on the web. A single conversation is not a helper.

**Three levels.**

- **Try freely.** A helper for your own work, on approved services and your own access, that drafts, sorts,
  summarizes or reminds, using public or internal information or records your role already shows you.
- **Check first.** A helper for your own use that handles Confidential or Restricted information (Section 12), or
  whose output is used in a decision about a person, gets an automatic AI check of what it reads, keeps, sends and
  runs. The reviewer is flagged only when the check finds a concern.
- **Full review by a person.** Any helper that does one of the things listed below.

**Every church helper goes on the list.** The check writes one line in a shared catalog: what it does, what it
reads, which level. That is our running log; the reviewer sees new lines in a weekly digest. The list and church
accounts hold church helpers only; helpers for personal life belong in a personal account and never touch church
information.

**Learning comes first.** The setup session covers what a helper can reach, keep and do, so people can tell which
level they are in. For common routine jobs, the reviewer keeps a small set of tested starter helpers anyone may
copy.

**Who reviews.** The reviewer is the person [the elders] name to keep the approved-services list (Section 14),
with a backup. A helper built outside the church's AI account, on someone's own login, reaches only what they can
already see; anything that reaches further needs a shared key or a connected app, which the reviewer's quarterly
check of connected apps will show.

**Freedom from review is not freedom from the rules.** Personal tools still keep counseling apart, still keep the
limits on giving and children's information, and still need a person's approval before anything is sent or
changed.

**A helper or tool needs full review when it does any of these:**

- shows information to someone other than its builder,
- stores it somewhere others can reach (a shared drive or a code repository),
- holds a shared church key,
- runs on its own, with no person present, or
- combines information about people from more than one system, or works with children's care information, even
  if only its builder uses it.

A tool that runs again and again pulls only the records and details its job needs. Counts and lists are done by
ordinary software; AI is used only to understand the request or explain the result.

Person-level member or giving information never goes into a code repository, reviewed or not: access to a
repository is all or nothing, and its history is permanent.

**What review looks like.** Any builder first runs an independent AI privacy check against a standard checklist,
then a person accepts the result and records that acceptance with the tool; that check helps a builder who is
learning, and it does not replace the person. Tools that hold sensitive information or a shared key also need the
reviewer's sign-off. Tools that show or store individual giving, counseling, children's care or personnel records
also need a second person, another elder or a qualified outside reviewer. Tools that help make decisions about
people are also checked for anyone they might overlook or misjudge. Before a sensitive tool goes live or changes in
a big way, it is tested for accuracy, for access limits and for what happens when something fails.

**Builders also follow four habits:** keys limited to the tool's job, sample data while testing, approval before
anything writes, and a log from day one.

**Seeing what others built.** What people build goes in the shared catalog, and before a build the AI checks it
and points out anything similar or anything worth connecting to. A [monthly] show-and-tell does the same among
people. The aim is that good work spreads and nobody builds blind.

### Keys and sign-ins

A **key** is a credential that lets software into one of our systems. **Each person uses their own account by
default,** administrators included, kept in their own entry in the church password manager. A shared church key
is used only for work that must run with no person present, and lives on church hardware or in a shared church
password vault for the role that runs it. A key that reaches everyone's personal records (the member directory,
giving, payroll) stays behind a church-run service that gives each person only their own share and logs every
use.

## 14. Approved services

An **approved service** is an outside service that church information passes through or that acts for us:

- AI services on the church's business plan: [AI SERVICES], under these same rules
- **connectors** (links that let AI look things up in one of our systems under a person's own login), signed in
  per person
- **sign-in services** (companies that hold a person's logins so AI can reach several apps), only after review and
  only with their copy of our information switched off
- **AI running on church computers** (a "local" model), treated like any tool the church builds (Section 13): the
  same review and the same main rule. Running AI ourselves is not automatically safer; it moves the copy into our
  building and the upkeep onto us.
- meeting tools, for recording under Section 9

Skills, plugins, prompts and scripts that people build on top of approved services are not services and are not
listed; they fall under Section 13.

**Who keeps the list.** [The elders] name a reviewer ([NAME OR ROLE]) and a backup. The reviewer adds and removes
services; [the elders] see every change at the quarterly check and may strike any.

**A short vendor checklist** is filled in before approval and rechecked at renewal: does it train on our data, how
long does it keep it, who else handles it, can we delete it, how secure is it, and will it tell us of a breach.

## 15. Keeping it safe, honestly

**What the settings and access do.** Through its connections, AI sees only what the person's own login shows.
Connections are set to ask before changes where the service allows it, and that is checked when each is approved.
Our business plan does not train on our data, by contract. Sending a conversation to the AI company as feedback
(the thumbs up or down) is switched off for the whole church where the plan allows, because companies may keep
feedback for years. [If your plan has no automatic deletion schedule for conversations, say so here; that is why
the records paragraph below asks people to delete sensitive ones.]

**What rests on people.** For much else, the protection is the attention of the person approving. A rushed yes is
the weak point, so training covers what to look at before you approve. The person who pastes member information
into a free AI tool is our biggest realistic risk, which is why everyone gets a church account.

**One real risk to name.** An AI reading outside content (an email, a form, a web page) can meet text written to
trick it. We do not rely on the AI never being fooled. We design tools so that no single one combines wide access
to information, open internet access and the power to act, we check that when a tool is reviewed, and a person
approves every action.

**When something goes wrong.** Anyone who thinks information went somewhere it should not, a message went to the
wrong person, a key was exposed or a tool did harm reports it at once to the reviewer (or the backup). The
reviewer pauses the access involved, keeps the evidence, corrects what can be corrected, and with [the elders]
decides who needs to be told.

**A register of uses.** Beyond the list of services, we keep one register of the recurring ways AI is used: what
it does, who owns it, what information it touches, and when it was last reviewed.

**Records.** Conversations stay in our accounts until someone deletes them. Save what matters to [WHERE CHURCH
RECORDS LIVE]. A conversation that held children's care information or Restricted information is deleted when the
task is done; others are tidied as a habit. The quarterly check looks at both. When counsel says records must be
preserved, routine deletion pauses.

**Sources and copyright.** Quotations and sources are checked before use, licenses are respected, and AI output is
not assumed to be original.

## 16. Training, support and rollout

**Training.** Each person gets a one-on-one setup of about [45 minutes] that ends with a first real project from
their own work, and covers what a helper can reach, keep and do. One group session covers these rules. A
[monthly] show-and-tell shares what people have built. A short refresher each year walks through real situations:
the wrong account, the wrong recipient, a deceptive email, an answer that is confidently wrong.

**Support.** The AI's built-in church guide answers "how do I" and "am I allowed to" first. Then the staff
champion ([NAME OR ROLE]). The reviewer handles reviews and new services.

**Rollout.** Every staff member, part-time included, is offered a church account, in waves. In three phases, under
one vote, so each step starts only once the one before it is working:

- **At adoption:** church accounts and these rules; everyday drafting and research; helpers at the try-freely
  level; meeting summaries; links to [CHURCH MANAGEMENT SYSTEM] for staff under their own login; links to

## 17. Questions for counsel

These are legal questions this policy does not settle on its own, to put to a lawyer before adoption:

  should shape what we record.
- Whether federal health privacy law (HIPAA) applies to our counseling.
- How [STATE]'s consumer privacy law applies to a church (many such laws exclude nonprofits, which is not the
  same as no privacy duties at all).
- What legal duties apply when AI handles information about children and minors.
- Beyond HIPAA, which [STATE] confidentiality laws apply to our counseling ministry and to AI help with case notes.

For our insurance broker: whether the church's existing coverage includes AI-related privacy incidents,
cyberattacks, and mistakes made through AI-enabled processes.
